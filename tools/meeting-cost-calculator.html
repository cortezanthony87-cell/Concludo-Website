What a meeting really costs

A meeting has a price whether or not anyone writes it down. It is the number of people in the room, multiplied by what an hour of their time is worth, multiplied by how long you keep them there. Run it every week and the figure quietly compounds into one of the larger line items in how your week is spent.

For consultants and property professionals, that hourly figure is not abstract. It is a billed rate, or the internal cost of a salaried colleague, and every hour spent in a room is an hour not spent on client work. The calculator above is deliberately blunt about this. It does not judge the meeting. It just shows you the number, so you can decide whether the meeting is worth it.

In a 2022 study for Otter.ai, Professor Steven Rogelberg of the University of North Carolina at Charlotte estimated that organisations invest roughly US$80,000 per professional employee each year in meetings, with about US$25,000 of that spent on meetings staff themselves considered unnecessary. The same survey found professionals were spending close to 18 hours a week in meetings.

Source: Rogelberg, S. for Otter.ai, "The Cost of Unnecessary Meeting Attendance," 2022. Figures are in US dollars and are indicative of a US sample, not an Australian benchmark.

The number is not the point. The decision is.

Seeing that a routine weekly slot costs several thousand dollars a year is not an argument for cancelling meetings. Plenty of them earn their keep many times over. The point is that once you can see the price, you can ask the fair question of any meeting: is it worth this? Three checks decide it.

  • There is a decision to make or a problem to solve, not just information to share.
  • The people in the room are the ones who can actually make that call.
  • Everyone leaves knowing the next action and who owns it.

That third point is where most of the money leaks. A meeting can be sharp in the room and still produce nothing, because the conversation never turns into owned, written actions. Rogelberg's survey found that most employees would feel comfortable skipping meetings entirely if clear notes were shared with them afterwards, which tells you how much of a meeting's value lives in the follow up rather than the room.
