02 The Ten Questions

Ten questions to ask before you send the invite.

Most meetings fail before they start: no clear purpose, the wrong tool, the wrong people in the room. Run down this list before the invite goes out. If a meeting still earns its place, you will run a far better one. If it does not, you have just handed a roomful of people their afternoon back. Fewer, smaller, shorter, better prepared.

Start with the one-question test: will people's responses to each other change the outcome?

This single test sits above the other ten. A meeting is worth holding only when the live back-and-forth changes what gets decided. If the outcome would be the same whether or not people can react to one another, you do not need a meeting. You need a clear piece of writing.

→ Yes, it will change the outcome. Meet. → No, it will not. Write it instead.

The ten, in order.

Work top to bottom. The first three decide whether to meet at all. The next four shape who comes and how they arrive. The last three protect the time, the consent and the record. Skip none of them.

01 · Purpose

Can I write the purpose and the outcome in one sentence each?

If you cannot state both in a single sentence, you are not ready to invite anyone. The purpose is why you are meeting. The outcome is what must be true when you leave.

What good looks like: "Decide the new price for the flagship product, and leave with a figure and an effective date."
02 · Right call

Will interaction genuinely change the outcome?

The one-question test, applied to this specific meeting. Discussion earns its cost when a decision needs judgement, when the topic is contested, or when people build on each other in real time. Otherwise, it is a memo.

What good looks like: a decision or debate that no document could resolve on its own.
03 · Right tool

Would a memo, email or short recording be faster and kinder?

One-way information transfer, routine status, or a decision already made: none of these need a meeting. Announce it honestly in writing, and give the time back. Meet only when live interaction is the right tool.

What good looks like: the meeting survives because writing genuinely could not do the job.
04 · Decision-maker

Is the person who actually decides in the room?

A meeting without its decision-maker is a rehearsal. Name the person accountable for the outcome, and confirm they can attend and have had time to prepare. If they cannot, move the meeting.

What good looks like: one named owner of the decision, present and prepared.
05 · Smallest group

Who is the smallest group that can reach the outcome?

Every acceptance interrupts someone's day. Invite the fewest people who can achieve the purpose, and tell optional invitees plainly that they are optional. Circulate the outcome to everyone who merely needs to know.

What good looks like: a group small enough to actually decide something.
06 · Agenda

Is every agenda item a question with a time limit?

Topics drift; questions get answered. Frame each item as the question the meeting must answer, put a time against it, and work them in order of importance, not ease. No agenda, no meeting.

What good looks like: "Which of the three options do we choose? (15 min)" not "Pricing."
07 · Pre-reading

What must people read first, and will they actually read it?

Preparation beats improvisation. Attach the brief, set the expectation that it is read, and consider opening the meeting with a few minutes of silent reading so debate starts from shared context rather than a cold start.

What good looks like: a short brief with the options, the data and a recommendation.
08 · Timing and length

Is this at the right time, for the shortest workable length?

Put decision-heavy meetings where energy is highest, usually earlier in the day. Default to 25 or 50 minutes instead of 30 or 60 so back-to-back sessions get breathing room, and protect meeting-free blocks for concentrated work.

What good looks like: 25 or 50 minutes, deliberately placed, deep-work time defended.
09 · Consent

If we record or use a note-taker, is consent arranged?

Consent comes before capture, every time. If the meeting will be recorded or run through a note-taker, arrange and state consent as part of the logistics, before anyone starts talking. It is a courtesy and, in most places, a requirement.

What good looks like: consent settled in the invite, not sprung in the room.
10 · The record

Who captures decisions, and where does the summary go within 24 hours?

The summary is the product; the meeting was only the factory. Name who captures decisions and actions live, each with an owner and a date, and decide before you start where the summary lands within 24 hours, somewhere the team actually looks.

What good looks like: decisions, actions, owners and dates, sent by tomorrow.
Fewer. Smaller. Shorter. Better prepared.
